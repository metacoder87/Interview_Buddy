## Q3: Walk me through Sidekick LLM
Sidekick LLM is a privacy-first desktop knowledge system for local LLMs via Ollama. The design decision that defines it: the product isn't the chat window, it's the corpus — persistent conversations, a de-duplicated memory of personal facts recalled by relevance rather than recency, and domain "Experts" built from your own PDFs. Engineering highlights I'm proud of: federated Expert consultation where one query searches multiple vaults and the prompt explicitly asks the model to surface disagreements instead of flattening them, with a grounding badge per answer; and an embedding contract treated as a load-bearing invariant — provenance on every vector, dimension-matched visibility rules, so retrieval degrades loudly instead of silently. It's Electron + React + SQLite in WAL mode with FTS5, fully offline.

## Q4: What was the hardest technical problem in Sidekick LLM?
Silent retrieval degradation. Vector search will happily return confidently wrong results when embeddings drift or a model swap changes vector dimensions — and users never know. I made correctness an invariant instead of a hope: every stored vector carries its embedding model and dimension, the write boundary rejects empty vectors, retrieval excludes and reports mismatched vectors rather than scoring them zero, and existing vaults are repairable through in-place re-indexing. The general lesson I carry into AI work: probabilistic components need deterministic guardrails around their inputs and outputs, and honest failure reporting beats blanket success.

## Q5: Walk me through SYNAPSE
SYNAPSE is a local-first job-intelligence platform. Six source adapters — USAJobs, Adzuna, Jooble, Greenhouse, Lever, RSS — normalize into one Pydantic model; a deterministic regex kill switch discards obvious mismatches before I spend embedding compute; MiniLM embeddings score alignment against my profile; PostgreSQL is the relational truth with ChromaDB keyed by the same UUIDs. On demand, a CrewAI crew on local Ollama — Scout, Networker, Fact-Checker — produces a company dossier where claims carry evidence references, numeric literals are checked deterministically, a temperature-zero verifier returns supported/contradicted/insufficient verdicts, and Python renders the final document rather than the model. Freshness is enforced three ways: daily expiry, a 12-hour heartbeat probing live URLs, weekly purge across both stores. ## Q6: How do you keep an LLM from hallucinating in a system like SYNAPSE?
Four layers. One, constrain where the model operates — it generates claims inside a stage, not the whole document. Two, make claims checkable — every claim carries evidence references and numeric literals are validated by deterministic code, not another model call. Three, use a verifier with a restricted output space — supported, contradicted, or insufficient at temperature zero; "insufficient" is a first-class answer. Four, keep final rendering deterministic — Python assembles the cited dossier from approved claims only. The same principle gates my application documents: only facts in an approved ledger can become candidate claims.

## Q7: Walk me through Investment Matrix
It's a self-hosted crypto research and paper-trading platform where nothing leaves the machine. Multi-exchange WebSocket streamers push ticks into Redis Streams with crash-safe consumption — pending-message recovery plus dead-letter queue — into TimescaleDB hypertables. The key architectural rule: the LLM is off the trade path. A deterministic millisecond-level formula engine — VWAP, CVD, ATR, RSI — makes every entry decision, and entries are validated three ways before execution: a seven-day backtest, a thesis-geometry simulation of the exact TP/SL being traded, and a guardrail stack with fractional-Kelly sizing from realized win rates. Losing strategies automatically size down because every prediction is scored against realized prices. Alongside trading, a seven-agent research organization on isolated Celery queues maintains a versioned strategy playbook with champion/challenger shadow evaluation and out-of-sample promotion gates.

## Q8: What was the hardest problem in Investment Matrix?
Backpressure between ingestion and storage at tick rates. A slow writer can't drop ticks, but an unbounded buffer is just deferred failure. The answer was Redis Streams as the isolation layer — streamers write and move on; consumers track pending messages with recovery plus a dead-letter queue for poison entries; TimescaleDB hypertables absorb the writes with 1m/5m rollups. I also had to decide what "correct" means when the market doesn't wait: intrabar high/low wick detection for entries and stops with conservative whipsaw handling, and fill simulation derived from stored bid/ask half-spreads instead of assuming mid-price fills. The lesson: in event-driven systems, define your consistency boundaries explicitly — exactly-once where it matters, at-least-once plus idempotent writes where it doesn't.

## Q10: Walk me through PyHoldem Pro
A quantitative poker training platform: concurrent CLI simulator plus React dashboard over FastAPI and PostgreSQL, with real-time EV-based coaching — every human decision point is graded against mathematical baselines and the EV loss in big blinds comes back immediately. Three problems I'd highlight. One: Monte Carlo equity computation was blocking the ASGI event loop — thousands of simulated deals per range query freezing WebSockets; I built a compressed lookup table with a fast hand-evaluation module, cached identical board/hand combos, and prefer exact enumeration when cards-to-come are low, taking it from seconds to single-digit milliseconds. Two: nine-handed ICM is 362,880 permutations — I wrote an O(2^n·n) memoized recursion with dynamic programming for larger fields, under five milliseconds. Three: client-side quiz cheating via inspectable state — solved with server-owned validation where correct answers never reach the frontend. The whole thing is backed by 630-plus passing tests.

## Q11: Walk me through the Qdrant Search Console
A document-first semantic search system over PDFs, HTML, and text. FastAPI backend, sentence-transformers embeddings into a disk-persistent Qdrant collection, React frontend with PDF.js rendering. The core engineering contribution is hit aggregation — raw ANN returns scattered chunks from one document, so I group chunk hits by doc_id, average similarity per document, and sort at the document level; big documents stop drowning results. Two things I'd emphasize for an AI-engineering panel: deterministic seeding for CI — a DEBUG-gated utility drops, recreates, and reseeds the collection with an exact ten-point dataset so test state is reproducible — and a strict human-in-the-loop feedback loop where downvotes require an explanation, because unlabeled negative examples are worthless for fine-tuning.

## Q15: Tell me about a time something failed or you found a bug in production-shaped code
S: In SYNAPSE, job records live in two stores — PostgreSQL for truth, ChromaDB for vectors, keyed by shared UUIDs. A permanent-delete path was removing the Postgres row but not always the vector, so deleted jobs could resurface in semantic search.
T: I owned data consistency across both stores; it had to be fixed without a manual cleanup script users would have to run.
A: I introduced tombstones for deletions plus a vector-cleanup retry record — the delete is durable first, vector removal retries until confirmed, and the weekly freshness purge reconciles anything still stale in both stores together.
R: The two stores can no longer drift on deletes, and the reconciliation pass made the failure mode self-healing instead of something I'd discover from a user report.

## Q19: How do you design a RAG system?
I'd walk it as five decisions. One, chunking strategy matched to the content — for my project docs I split on section headers first because that's where the evidence lives, then size-cap at paragraph boundaries with overlap. Two, embedding model chosen by measured latency and quality on your actual query distribution, not benchmarks — I run nomic-embed-text locally through Ollama when privacy or cost matters. Three, retrieval architecture: dense vectors for semantics, but aggregate to the right unit of meaning — in Qdrant Search that was chunk hits averaged up to document level so big documents stop dominating. Four, grounding discipline: retrieved context gets cited, and generation is constrained to what's retrievable — my overlay literally marks each bullet as grounded-in-your-docs versus general knowledge. Five, evaluation before you trust it: golden question sets with measured retrieval hit-rate, like the ten-point deterministic seed dataset I use for CI in Qdrant Search.

## Q20: How do you evaluate generative models?
Three layers from my actual workflow. One, rubric-based evals — when I evaluated Codex and Claude for code generation and deterministic JSON output, I wrote explicit rubrics per capability so "better" was a score, not a vibe. Two, constrained-output testing — if the model must emit JSON or structured claims, test that contract hard: schema validation, retry behavior, and what happens on invalid output; in Investment Matrix the LLM runs under a JSON contract with prompt retries and agent validation. Three, golden sets with deterministic checks where possible — SYNAPSE's dossier pipeline checks numeric literals by code and uses a temperature-zero verifier with a three-way verdict space, so evaluation is reproducible run to run. And I track it over time: per-strategy scorecards in Investment Matrix feed position sizing, which means the eval loop changes real behavior, not just a dashboard.

## Q21: Local LLMs versus cloud APIs — how do you decide?
I default local when any of these are true: the data is sensitive — my trading strategies and job-search history never leave the machine; latency has to be predictable — a warm local model on my RTX 5080 gives me first tokens in one to three seconds with no network variance; or cost compounds — Investment Matrix's nightly analyst and SYNAPSE's dossier crew run constantly, so zero per-token pricing matters. Cloud wins when I need the strongest reasoning for a one-off hard problem, or when the team is smaller than my context window — which is why everything I build keeps an escape hatch: Ollama as the default provider with the same JSON contracts a cloud API would use, so switching is a config change. The anti-pattern I avoid is local-by-default-everywhere: for interactive chat on weak hardware, a small fast model locally beats a big slow one, and that's a measured decision — I benchmark TTFT and tokens-per-second on the actual machine before choosing.

## Q22: When would you use multi-agent systems? And when wouldn't you?
I'd use them when the work decomposes into roles with different information needs or different trust levels — Investment Matrix's research team is the example: Scout selects coins, Deep Research builds dossiers, Backtest Lab sweeps parameters on historic data, Strategy Scientist converts validated findings into a versioned playbook, and a Risk Officer sits between research and live trading. Two rules I enforce from that build: agents get isolated queues so research can never delay trading, and autonomy is tiered — playbook promotions are manual at tier zero, automatic only at tier one after out-of-sample gates pass. I wouldn't use them when a single prompt with good structure does the job — extra agents multiply your failure surface and your eval burden. SYNAPSE's crew is deliberately three agents, not ten: Scout, Networker, Fact-Checker, because those are the three distinct information roles the dossier actually needs.

## Q23: How do you choose a vector database?
For my scale — thousands of chunks, single user or small team — I'd be honest that the differentiator is operational, not raw ANN performance: persistence model, filtering quality, and consistency story with your relational store. That's why Qdrant in Docker with disk-persistent volumes won for both Qdrant Search and SYNAPSE — same UUIDs across Postgres and ChromaDB or Qdrant, deterministic seeding utilities for CI, and a clear path to hybrid search when I need it. The consistency lesson from running dual stores: define the source of truth explicitly (Postgres), key vectors by the same IDs, make deletes tombstone-plus-retry rather than assume atomicity across systems, and add a reconciliation pass. At larger scale I'd evaluate on filtered-search latency with my real query mix, replication story, and what happens to embeddings when dimensions change — which is why Sidekick LLM stores embedding provenance per vector.

## Q24: Design a system to match job candidates to postings at scale
I've built exactly this as SYNAPSE, so I'd design it from that experience. Ingestion: adapter pattern per source normalizing into one schema — six sources in my build — because every provider's payload is different and you don't want that mess leaking into ranking. Screening before spend: a deterministic rule gate kills obvious mismatches cheaply; embeddings are the expensive step so they run on survivors only. Ranking: semantic similarity against the candidate profile, but blended with role-lane, skill, seniority, freshness, and source-quality signals — pure cosine ranking over-weights vocabulary match. Freshness is a first-class subsystem: daily expiry on closing dates, heartbeat probes of live URLs, periodic purge across stores. Then the human-in-the-loop layer: lifecycle tracking, reversible dismissals, structured feedback that can retrain or rescore later, and on-demand deep research — in my case an agent crew producing fact-checked dossiers with claim-level verification. I'd add for scale: idempotent ingestion keyed by posting identity, a scoring service decoupled from the API tier so rescoring is a batch job, and evals — useful-job rate and stale-posting rate as the metrics that tell you ranking actually improved.

## Q25: Design a real-time market data pipeline
From Investment Matrix. Ingestion: one WebSocket streamer per exchange pushing standardized ticks into Redis Streams — streams give backpressure isolation, pending-message recovery, and a dead-letter queue for poison entries, so a slow consumer never drops market data. Storage: TimescaleDB hypertables for raw ticks with continuous aggregates rolling to 1m/5m; hot reads go through Redis first for sub-second latency. Decisioning: the key rule is that the LLM stays off the trade path — a deterministic formula engine makes entry decisions, validated three ways (backtest, thesis-geometry simulation of the exact TP/SL, guardrail stack with fractional-Kelly sizing from realized win rates). Learning loop: every prediction scored against realized prices feeds per-strategy scorecards that resize positions automatically. Observability is non-negotiable at this layer — writer throughput and lag sparklines, stream depths, queue depths on a live ops page, because in event-driven systems the first symptom of failure is a depth graph going vertical.
